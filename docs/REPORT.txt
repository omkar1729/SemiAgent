================================================================================
 SemiAgent — Automated Wafer Defect Root Cause Analysis
 EXPERIMENT REPORT (in plain language)
================================================================================

A few terms used throughout (kept simple):
  - Wafer bin map : a grid picture of a silicon wafer where each cell is a good or
                    failing chip. The PATTERN of failing cells (a ring, a center
                    blob, a scratch...) hints at what went wrong in the factory.
  - Defect type   : the name of that failure pattern (Center, Donut, Edge-Loc,
                    Edge-Ring, Local, Near-Full, Random, Scratch, or a mix).
  - "Detection"   : look at the wafer picture and name the defect pattern.
  - "Retrieval"   : pull up the most relevant technical documents (like a librarian).
  - "Diagnosis"   : write the root-cause explanation, grounded in those documents.
  - Accuracy      : how often the top guess is exactly correct (1.0 = 100%).
  - Macro-F1      : an accuracy-like score that treats every defect class equally,
                    so a model can't look good just by nailing the common classes.
  - p-value       : a statistics check of whether a difference is real or just luck.
                    Smaller = more trustworthy. Below 0.05 = "probably a real effect".

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. WHAT THE SYSTEM DOES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Given a wafer picture (and optionally a short note from an engineer), the system
produces a structured "why did this fail" report in three steps run one after another:

  STEP 1 - Detection : an AI looks at the wafer picture and names the defect pattern.
  STEP 2 - Retrieval : the system searches a library of ~1,000 technical documents
                       and pulls the 5 most relevant ones for that defect.
  STEP 3 - Diagnosis : a large language model reads those documents plus the
                       engineer's note and writes the report: a summary, 2-3 ranked
                       likely causes (each citing a document), and the measurements
                       an engineer should take to confirm.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2. SETUP (so the results can be reproduced)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
- Computer: Windows PC, 12 CPU cores, one NVIDIA RTX 2060 graphics card (6 GB).
- All random choices are fixed to a fixed "seed" (42) so re-running gives the same
  result.
- Every experiment's numbers are recorded automatically in MLflow (a tracking tool)
  and saved as spreadsheet (CSV) files under evaluation/results/.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3. THE DATA
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
- We use MixedWM38: 38,015 wafer pictures, each 52x52 cells.
- 38 possible labels: "Normal" (no defect), 8 single defect patterns, and 29
  combinations of those 8.
- The raw file stores, for each wafer, an 8-slot checklist of which BASIC defect
  types are present (cell values 0=outside the wafer, 1=good chip, 2=failing chip).
  We turn that checklist into the proper 38-way label. The slot order is the dataset
  authors' OFFICIAL mapping (Junliangwangdhu/WaferMap), double-checked against the
  dataset's documented row ranges:
     slot 0=Center 1=Donut 2=Edge-Loc 3=Edge-Ring 4=Local 5=Near-Full 6=Scratch 7=Random
  ("Random" only ever appears on its own; "Scratch" appears in 18 of the 38 patterns.)
- Split 70/15/15, balanced by defect type: train 26,610 / validation 5,702 /
  test 5,703 (the test set is held back and used ONLY for final scoring).

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4. THE DOCUMENT LIBRARY (used for retrieval)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
~1,000 short technical documents, from three sources:
  - 776 research-paper summaries (from arXiv)
  - 200 patent texts about semiconductor manufacturing (via Google Patents)
  -  24 hand-authored seed reference notes (3 for each of the 8 defect types) - written
        within the project (synthetic), not externally expert-validated. They also
        serve as the "relevant" set when we test the search.
Search method ("hybrid"): combine keyword search (BM25) and meaning search (dense
embeddings) and merge them with Reciprocal Rank Fusion.

================================================================================
 EXPERIMENT 1 - CAN IT NAME THE DEFECT? (Detection)
================================================================================
What we tested: how often the system names the correct defect pattern, on 500
unseen test pictures, comparing three detectors on the SAME pictures.

  CLIP, off the shelf : a general image AI used as-is (NO wafer training). We hand it
                        the 38 defect descriptions as text and it matches each picture
                        to the closest description.
  ViT, untrained      : our image model with a fresh, random final layer (control).
  ViT, trained by us  : the same model after training on the 26,610 training wafers.

We report two scores: top-1 accuracy and macro-F1 (equal weight to every class).

RESULTS (500 test pictures):
  Detector              top-1 accuracy    macro-F1
  CLIP, off the shelf       0.050           0.008   - fails; wafer maps are nothing
                                                      like the photos CLIP was built
                                                      for, so it collapses onto one class
  ViT, untrained            0.044           0.008   - chance level, as expected
  ViT, trained by us        0.984           0.982   - 100% on every single-defect type

Training detail: the trained ViT (a ViT-Base model) learned over 10 passes;
validation accuracy rose 95.1 -> 97.3 -> 97.3 -> 98.0 -> 98.1 -> 98.3 -> 98.5 ->
98.7 -> 98.7 -> 98.8%. Bottom line: training our own detector took it from ~5% to 98%.
Its 0.982 macro-F1 is an apples-to-apples match to the published WaferSegClassNet
benchmark (98.2%).

================================================================================
 EXPERIMENT 2 - CAN IT FIND THE RIGHT DOCUMENTS? (Retrieval)
================================================================================
What we tested: when asked about a defect, does the search return relevant documents?
We measured two ways, over the 8 defect-type questions:
  - Precision@5: how many of the top-5 returned docs are truly on-topic (needs the
    24 seed reference notes as the "relevant" set).
  - Retrieval relevance (cosine similarity): how semantically close the top-5 docs
    are to the question, 0-1 (this is the thesis's stated retrieval metric).

RESULTS:
  Precision@5 (average over 8 questions):
     Keyword-only (sparse)  0.525   <- best
     Meaning-only (dense)   0.475
     Hybrid (both merged)   0.450
  Retrieval relevance (mean cosine similarity of hybrid top-5 to the question): 0.671

What it means: about half of the top-5 are spot-on (P@5 ~0.5), and the retrieved docs
are moderately on-topic by cosine similarity (0.67). Both are below the optimistic
targets (P@5 ~0.85 / cosine >0.85): our defects describe similar things (e.g. "Donut"
and "Edge-Ring" are both rings), so the search mixes them up, and on this small judged
set the plain keyword search slightly beats the hybrid. For writing the diagnosis this
is fine - the AI still gets 2-3 strongly relevant documents plus related context.
(Caveat: the seed notes are both IN the corpus AND the relevance ground truth, so this
P@5 measures recovery of planted relevant docs rather than independent human judgment.)

================================================================================
 EXPERIMENT 3 - HOW GOOD IS THE WRITTEN DIAGNOSIS, AND WHAT DRIVES IT?
================================================================================
One experiment, scored on the same pictures in a single pass, replacing the three
separate diagnosis/ablation tests from the earlier version (no duplicated work).

How we measured: an AI "grader" (GPT-4o-mini) scores each report 1 (poor) to 5
(excellent) on five qualities, averaged and put on a 0-10 scale:
   1. Correct cause   2. Backed by evidence   3. Technically correct
   4. Thorough        5. Actionable
We ran it on a balanced sample at three sizes (10, 30, 50 - each a subset of the next).

A) THE FULL SYSTEM'S QUALITY (N=50)
   Full system "B0" = trained ViT detector + no engineer note + hybrid search +
   GPT-4o-mini writer. Average score: 9.60 out of 10.
   By quality: Correct cause 5.0 | Backed by evidence 5.0 | Technically correct 5.0 |
               Actionable 5.0 | Thorough 4.0 (the weakest - reports sometimes give
               fewer distinct causes).

B) DOES THE CHOICE OF DETECTOR / NOTE MATTER? (a 3 x 2 grid, score /10, N=50)
                         no note    with note
     CLIP detector         9.24       9.15
     trained ViT (=B0)     9.60       9.39
     untrained ViT         9.89       9.42
   - The detector barely changes the written report's score. Surprisingly the
     UNTRAINED detector scores highest with no note (9.89). This is a quirk, not a
     real win: the untrained model keeps guessing the same (wrong) defect, the AI then
     writes a coherent, well-grounded report for that guess, and with no note nothing
     reveals the mistake - so the grader (which rewards coherence and citations, not
     whether the defect NAME was right) scores it high. A correct note then CONTRADICTS
     the wrong guess and the score falls to 9.42.
   - The engineer note slightly LOWERED the score on average (no-note 9.58 vs note
     9.32; p<0.001), driven by that conflict; with the correct (trained) detector the
     note is roughly neutral.

C) WHICH PART OF THE PIPELINE MATTERS MOST? (knock-out test, vs B0)
   Change ONE thing from the full system and re-score, at three sample sizes (each a
   subset of the next). Scores are out of 10:

     Setup (what changed)                   N=10    N=30    N=50
     Full system (B0)                        9.52    9.57    9.60
     Use perfect (oracle) detection          9.52    9.55    9.57
     GPT given short (truncated) docs         9.52    9.52    9.51
     Weaken search to keyword-only           9.04    9.09    9.22
     Swap writer to SemiKong-8B              9.28    9.33    9.33
     Remove document search (keep the LLM)   7.52    7.79    7.84
     Replace the LLM with a fixed template   7.36    7.56    7.52

   Is the change real? (Wilcoxon p vs B0, at N=10 / N=30 / N=50)
     oracle detection      1.00  / 0.74    / 0.59     -> never significant
     GPT truncated docs    1.00  / 0.48    / 0.16     -> never significant
     keyword-only search   0.28  / 0.009   / 0.007    -> real from N=30
     SemiKong writer       0.25  / 0.020   / <0.001   -> real from N=30
     no document search    0.002 / <0.001  / <0.001   -> always real
     template (no LLM)     0.002 / <0.001  / <0.001   -> always real

   What it means:
   - The TWO things that matter most are the LLM writer and the document search, at
     every sample size. Replacing the LLM with a no-reasoning template drops the score
     the most (to ~7.4-7.5), and removing the document search is almost as damaging
     (to ~7.5-7.8). Both are large and significant at all N. So report quality rests on
     BOTH the language model's reasoning AND the retrieved documents.
   - PERFECT detection barely helps (9.60 -> 9.57, never significant): detector accuracy
     is not what drives the written report's quality.
   - The SMALL effects (keyword-only search, SemiKong writer) only reach significance
     from N=30 onward; their non-significance at N=10 is a sample-size (power) artifact,
     not absence of an effect - the direction is consistent across all three sizes.

D) IS THE SPECIALISED MODEL BETTER, AND WAS THE EARLIER COMPARISON FAIR? (N=50)
   SemiKong-8B (a semiconductor-specialised model) has a smaller memory for input
   text, so earlier it was given shorter document snippets than GPT-4o-mini - an
   unfair handicap. So we re-ran GPT-4o-mini on the SAME shortened documents:
     GPT-4o-mini, full docs (B0)        9.60
     GPT-4o-mini, shortened docs        9.51   (shortening barely hurt GPT; p=0.16)
     SemiKong-8B, shortened docs        9.33
   Fair comparison on identical shortened docs: GPT-4o-mini 9.51 vs SemiKong 9.33,
   p=0.008 -> GPT-4o-mini is genuinely better, NOT just because it got longer docs.
   (One caveat remained - the grader is itself GPT-4o-mini - which Experiment 4 resolves.)

E) HOW FAST IS IT? (end-to-end latency, N=50)
   Average time to process one wafer end-to-end: 4,557 ms (~4.6 seconds), split as
     detection 88 ms  +  retrieval 18 ms  +  diagnosis (LLM) 4,451 ms.
   So ~98% of the time is the language model writing the report over the internet; the
   image and search steps are negligible. A faster GPU would not help; only a faster /
   higher-throughput LLM service would.

ARE THE DETECTOR/NOTE DIFFERENCES REAL? (statistics, N=50)
   CLIP vs trained ViT        p<0.001   ViT_untrained vs trained  p=0.012
   CLIP vs untrained ViT      p<0.001   note vs no-note           p<0.001
   All these gaps are tiny in size (every setup scores 9.1-9.9 out of 10); the only
   LARGE, decision-changing effects are removing the LLM or the document search.

================================================================================
 EXPERIMENT 4 - IS THE AI GRADER ITSELF BIASED? (Multi-judge cross-check)
================================================================================
A fair worry: in Experiment 3 the grader was GPT-4o-mini, the SAME model that writes
the "full system" reports - so it might simply favour its own writing style when we
compare it to SemiKong. To test this, we re-graded the SAME reports with FOUR
different graders and checked whether they agree.

Setup: 20 wafers, each written up twice (once by GPT-4o-mini, once by SemiKong-8B),
then every report scored by four graders using the identical rubric:
  GPT-4o-mini (the original grader), Claude (Sonnet 4.5), Gemini (2.5 Flash), and
  SemiKong-8B itself.

RESULT - average score (out of 10) each grader gave to each writer's reports:
  Report written by    Claude   Gemini   GPT-4o-mini   SemiKong
  GPT-4o-mini           9.10     9.92        9.64         5.44
  SemiKong-8B           8.50     9.06        9.24         5.00

Does each grader rank GPT-4o-mini's reports above SemiKong's? (gap, and is it real?)
  Grader         GPT minus SemiKong    Real difference?
  GPT-4o-mini         +0.40            YES (p=0.001)
  Claude              +0.60            YES (p=0.004)
  Gemini              +0.86            YES (p=0.004)
  SemiKong            +0.44            almost (p=0.063)

What it means:
  - ALL FOUR graders - including two completely independent ones (Claude, Gemini) and
    even SemiKong grading itself - rank GPT-4o-mini's reports above SemiKong's. So the
    "GPT-4o-mini writes better diagnoses than SemiKong-8B" finding (Experiment 3) is
    NOT an artifact of GPT grading its own work.
  - In fact the GPT grader gives the SMALLEST pro-GPT gap (+0.40); Claude (+0.60) and
    Gemini (+0.86) favour GPT even more. If anything the GPT grader was conservative,
    not self-flattering.
  - Even SemiKong, grading itself, still prefers the GPT-written reports.

Two caveats about the graders themselves:
  - They differ in strictness: Gemini (9.49) and GPT-4o-mini (9.44) are lenient,
    Claude (8.80) a little stricter, and SemiKong-8B is MUCH harsher (5.22 average) -
    it reads the rubric very strictly. So the absolute "9.6 out of 10" is grader-
    specific; only the RELATIVE ranking (GPT > SemiKong) travels across graders.
  - The three big graders agree moderately with each other (rank correlation ~0.3-0.5);
    SemiKong-8B is an outlier (weak correlation, ~4 points lower scale), i.e. not a
    reliable grader on its own - we include it only as a fourth independent vote.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5. SPEED NOTES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
- Training the detector ran on the graphics card: 10 passes over 26,610 pictures in
  roughly 1.5 hours.
- The diagnosis experiment is limited by the LLM service's 200,000 words-per-minute
  ("tokens per minute") cap, not the local machine. We made grading ~3x cheaper by
  asking the grader for all five scores in ONE request instead of five, which keeps us
  under that cap. The 50-picture study then runs in ~25 minutes.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
6. MAIN TAKEAWAYS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. Our trained detector reads wafers very well: 98% correct / 0.982 macro-F1 on unseen
   test pictures (100% on each single-defect type), versus ~5% (0.008 F1) off the shelf.
2. The written report's quality rests on TWO components: the LLM's reasoning and the
   retrieved documents. Removing either is the biggest, most reliable drop (to ~7.5-7.9
   out of 10) at every sample size; a no-LLM template is the single largest drop.
3. The detector's accuracy and the engineer's note do NOT meaningfully improve the
   report's quality score - the LLM + documents carry it. (The grader rewards a
   coherent, well-cited report even when the defect name handed to it is wrong.)
4. GPT-4o-mini beats the specialised SemiKong-8B even on identical (shortened)
   documents (9.51 vs 9.33, p=0.008) - a real model-quality gap, not an input handicap.
   This is confirmed by four independent graders (Experiment 4), so it is not an
   artifact of GPT grading its own work.
5. End-to-end latency is ~4.6 s/wafer, ~98% of it the LLM call.
6. Overall report quality is high (full system ~9.6 out of 10).

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
7. KNOWN LIMITATIONS (honest caveats)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
- Quality grading is by an LLM rubric, not human domain experts. The risk of the
  grader favouring its own family was tested directly (Experiment 4): four graders
  agree on the GPT>SemiKong ranking, so that specific concern is addressed - but
  human expert review remains future work and absolute scores are grader-specific.
- The diagnosis study covers the 8 single-defect classes (not the 29 mixed classes)
  and small picture sets (10-50), so treat its small effects as early signals; the
  largest effects (LLM and document-search removal) are the ones to trust most.
- Retrieval relevance fell short of the >85% target (cosine 0.671, P@5 ~0.5), and the
  retrieval ground truth (seed notes) is not independent human judgment.
- Detection (500 pictures) and search (8 questions) numbers are on larger sets.
